The game you're playing

Your class of 2027 is the first cohort to sit the new English Advanced syllabus. The four Year 12 focus areas are the same names you'll hear in class: Texts and human experiences, Textual conversations (Module A), Critical study of literature (Module B) and The craft of writing (Module C). The HSC is 100 marks over two papers, and three of the five sections are 20-mark essays. Get the essay machine right and you've covered 60% of the exam, plus most of your school assessments.

What markers actually reward

Markers use a 5-band criteria table for each 20-mark essay. The criteria are nearly identical across modules. The only thing that changes between bands is the adverb: describe → soundly → effectively → skilfully. So "how do I get 20?" really means "what does skilful look like?" Below is the 2024 HSC Module A table (paraphrased), translated into plain behaviour.

The 8 laws, from HSC marker feedback

Pulled from NESA's published "in better responses" and "areas to improve" notes for the 2025 English Advanced exam. These are the patterns that separate top essays.

1. Answer every part of the question. Better responses addressed all aspects of the question throughout, not just in the intro.
2. Analyse, never retell. "Moving beyond paraphrasing or summarising" and "rather than retelling plot events" appear in nearly every module's feedback.
3. Weave context in. Markers praised integrating contextual values into analysis; they criticised describing context separately.
4. Use a confident personal voice. "Confident personal voice and informed judgements" is the most repeated phrase in the 2025 Module B feedback.
5. Be clear, not fancy. Students were told to avoid overly complex or wordy language and use precise technical terms (form, genre, style).
6. Use a conceptual thesis. A thesis about an idea (power, endurance, conformity) rather than about plot. Don't reproduce a prepared response.
7. Integrate evidence. Quotes go inside your sentences and get analysed, not listed at the start or end of a paragraph.
8. Balance (Module A). Treat both texts evenly and evaluate how one responds to the other.

The formulas

Each part of an essay has a job. Each job has a formula. Learn the colours: they're used the same way in the model essay, the videos and the drills.

ThesisTopic sentence / roadmapEvidence + techniqueAnalysis: effect + ideaContextLink back
F0 · The whole essay
Essay = Intro + BP₁ + BP₂ + BP₃ + Conclusion

Three body paragraphs is the sweet spot for 40 minutes: enough to build an argument, few enough to go deep. Order them small → big, so your final paragraph carries your most sophisticated point (usually about structure or the whole text).

F1 · Thesis (the most important sentence you'll write)
Thesis = Composer + text + precise verb + Idea from the question + Complication
Insight = Idea + Tension   // "X, yet also Y" beats "X"

The complication is the secret. "Lawson shows the drover's wife is resilient" is a fact. "Lawson reveals that her resilience is paradoxical: the stoicism that lets her survive also erodes the self it protects" is an argument, and arguments are what get 17+.

✗ Band 3–4 thesis
"In The Drover's Wife, Henry Lawson shows many human experiences like loneliness and fear."
✓ Band 6 thesis
"Henry Lawson's 'The Drover's Wife' reveals that human endurance is fundamentally paradoxical: the same stoicism that allows an isolated individual to survive hardship also quietly erodes the self it protects."
F2 · Introduction (~150 words, 4 sentences)
Intro = S1 Thesis + S2 Context + S3 Roadmap (A, B, C) + S4 Answer the question's verb
  • S1: answer the question immediately. No "Since the dawn of time".
  • S2: one sentence of context that matters to your argument (when, where, why the composer wrote it).
  • S3: name your three sub-arguments in one sentence. These become your three topic sentences.
  • S4: if the question says "to what extent", give an extent ("largely", "only partly", "entirely"). If it says "evaluate", give a judgement.
F3 · Topic sentence
TS = Composer + verb + sub-argument (uses the question's keyword) + so that / to suggest…

Test: if a marker read only your topic sentences, would they get your whole argument? They should.

F4 · The analysis atom (repeat 3× per paragraph)
Quote × Technique → Effect on reader → Idea it builds

Read it like a function: input a quote, name the technique, output what it does to the reader and what idea that creates. Naming a technique with no effect scores almost nothing. "Technique spotting" is the most common Band 4 habit.

✗ Spotted, not analysed
"Lawson uses repetition in 'Bush all round—bush with no horizon'."
✓ Full atom
"The repetition in 'Bush all round—bush with no horizon' [evidence + technique] traps the reader in the same unbroken view [effect], the absent horizon becoming a metaphor for a life without prospect [idea]."
F5 · Body paragraph (~280 words)
BP = TS + Atom₁ + Atom₂ + Atom₃ + Context woven in + Link
  • Escalate: Atom₁ = a word or phrase. Atom₂ = a scene or image. Atom₃ = structure, form or a motif across the whole text.
  • Each quote proves a different slice of the topic sentence. Three quotes saying the same thing = one quote's worth of marks.
  • Context goes inside a sentence of analysis: "Writing amid X, Lawson…" not a separate history lesson.
  • Link: one evaluative sentence answering the question again, in light of what you just proved.
F6 · Conclusion (~100 words)
Concl = Thesis, reworded + A + B + C compressed into 1 sentence + So what? (why it matters now)

No new evidence. Never "In conclusion, I have shown". The "so what" is your last impression; make it a genuine thought about why the text still matters.

F7 · Planning (the 5 minutes that decide your mark)
Plan = Decode Q → Thesis → A, B, C → 3 quotes each → 1 context hook each

F8 · Rough mark model (my heuristic, for self-checking)
Mark ≈ Q·5 + A·6 + E·3 + C·3 + X·3  // each factor scored 0–1
  • Q question + thesis: answers this exact question, sustained to the end (5)
  • A analysis depth: effect + idea every time, never retell (6)
  • E evidence: well-chosen, varied, integrated (3)
  • C context and form: woven into analysis (3)
  • X expression: clear, controlled, confident voice (3)

NESA marks holistically, not by adding parts. Use this to find your weakest factor, then drill that one.

The analysis ladder

This is the single most useful idea on this page. There are six levels of talking about one quote. Most students stop at level 1 or 2. Band 6 sentences live at 4 and 5. Tap each rung to see the same quote analysed at that level.

"Bush all round—bush with no horizon, for the country is flat." · Henry Lawson, "The Drover's Wife" (1892)

Rule of thumb: every quote you use must reach at least level 3. Each paragraph should touch level 4 (context) once and end on level 5 (your judgement).

Texts reveal the contradictions in how individuals respond to hardship. To what extent is this true of your prescribed text?

Introduction · F2

Henry Lawson's 1892 short story "The Drover's Wife" reveals that human endurance is fundamentally paradoxical: the same stoicism that allows an isolated individual to survive hardship also quietly erodes the self it protects. Writing for The Bulletin during the economic depression of the 1890s, when the nationalist "bush legend" celebrated the self-reliant bushman, Lawson redirects that myth towards a woman whose resilience is both heroic and costly. Through his oppressive representation of the landscape, his restrained narrative voice and the story's ambivalent resolution, Lawson invites readers to admire his protagonist's strength while recognising it as a symptom of deprivation. The statement is therefore largely true, as the text exposes contradiction as the price of surviving a world that offers no alternatives.

Body paragraph 1 · Sub-argument A

Lawson first establishes that the bush provokes a contradictory response, since the environment that demands resilience simultaneously denies the protagonist any sense of a future. The story opens with an inventory of crude materials, "round timber, slabs, and stringy-bark", the list and present tense constructing a home that is functional yet emotionally bare. This flat, documentary style positions the reader as an observer of a life reduced to survival. The repetition in "Bush all round—bush with no horizon" intensifies this effect, trapping the reader in the same unbroken view, the absent horizon becoming a metaphor for a life without prospect. Endurance here is not chosen; it is imposed by a space that offers nowhere to go. The precise measurement "Nineteen miles to the nearest sign of civilization" quantifies her isolation, while the bathos of civilisation being merely "a shanty" deflates any romantic notion of the bush. Where Banjo Paterson's contemporaneous verse imagined the bush as a site of freedom and masculine adventure, Lawson's realism exposes its cost to those left behind. The landscape thus makes the drover's wife's resilience both necessary and imprisoning, revealing the first contradiction in her experience of hardship.

Body paragraph 2 · Sub-argument B

Lawson deepens this paradox through a restrained narrative voice that presents the protagonist's stoicism as admirable while hinting at the emotional losses it conceals. The understated declarative "She is used to being left alone" normalises abandonment, and the passive "left" quietly assigns responsibility to the absent husband even as the narrator refuses to judge him outright. This tension surfaces again in the concession "He is careless, but a good enough husband", where the conjunction "but" enacts her habit of minimising neglect, a coping mechanism that protects her by lowering her expectations. Most poignantly, the clichéd idiom "castles in the air" collapses into the stark finality of "long been dead", the funereal diction suggesting that endurance has required the death of her imagination. When the narrator exclaims "Heaven help her!" at her pleasure in fashion-plates, the outburst of pity invites readers to feel what she cannot afford to. Raised by Louisa Lawson, a feminist publisher, Lawson gives voice to the domestic labour that the masculine bush legend erased. Her stoicism is thus revealed as self-protection that simultaneously diminishes her, a contradiction the reader recognises even when she does not.

Body paragraph 3 · Sub-argument C (biggest idea: structure)

Finally, Lawson's ambivalent resolution suggests that the contradictions of endurance are passed between generations, so that survival becomes both a triumph and an inheritance of hardship. The night-long vigil against the snake frames the story as a test of endurance, and its episodic flashbacks, in which she "fought a flood" among other trials, accumulate to present her life as a cycle of battles rather than a narrative of progress. When the snake is finally killed, the climax dissolves into tenderness rather than celebration as "she hugs him to her worn-out breast"; the compound adjective "worn-out" reminds readers that her body has absorbed the cost of every victory. Tommy's vow, "Mother, I won't never go drovin'; blarst me if I do!", is both comic and ironic: his vernacular loyalty rejects his father's absence, yet signals that he is already being shaped by the same harsh code. The closing image of "sickly daylight" offers dawn without renewal, an unsettling closure that withholds catharsis. Lawson thus honours the protagonist's endurance while refusing to pretend it has set her free.

Conclusion · F6

Ultimately, "The Drover's Wife" demonstrates that human responses to hardship are rarely simple. Through a hostile landscape, a restrained narrative voice and an unresolved ending, Lawson portrays resilience as at once his protagonist's greatest strength and the measure of what she has lost. In doing so, he unsettles the celebratory bush mythology of his era and leaves modern readers with a sharper question: whether endurance should simply be admired, or whether the conditions that demand it should be changed.

Same quote, two bands

Band 3–4 version

"In The Drover's Wife, Lawson uses repetition in 'Bush all round—bush with no horizon' to show that the bush is big. This shows that she is lonely. This relates to human experiences because lots of people feel lonely."
  • "shows" twice, vague verbs
  • technique named, effect barely explained
  • idea is obvious ("lonely"), no tension
  • link to the question is generic
Band 6 version

"The repetition in 'Bush all round—bush with no horizon' traps the reader in the same unbroken view, the absent horizon becoming a metaphor for a life without prospect. Endurance here is not chosen; it is imposed by a space that offers nowhere to go."
  • effect on the reader ("traps the reader")
  • a second layer: horizon → metaphor for future
  • idea tied to the question's keyword (endurance / hardship)
  • a judgement with tension ("not chosen; imposed")

How the formula changes per module

Same engine, different fuel. The skeleton (F0–F6) never changes. What each module adds is one or two extra variables the marker is hunting for.

Paper 1 · Section II

Texts and human experiences

+ individual ↔ collective + emotions / qualities + paradox, agency vs conformity
  • The syllabus asks how texts represent identity, acceptance and growth, the tension between agency and conformity, and paradoxes of motivation and behaviour. Build your thesis around one of these.
  • Better 2025 responses showed how the text enriched the student's own understanding. End paragraphs with a personal reflection.
  • The question may include a stimulus. Use its words in your thesis.
Paper 2 · Section I

Module A: Textual conversations

+ Context₁ vs Context₂ + resonance (echo) + dissonance (clash) + how text₂ reframes text₁
  • Integrated paragraphs: each paragraph discusses both texts on one idea. Roughly half the evidence from each.
  • Paragraph formula: TS (idea across both) → Text 1 atoms → pivot word ("Where…", "Conversely…", "Extending this…") → Text 2 atoms → evaluate the conversation.
  • Explain why they differ: different contexts, values and forms.
  • Use the module's own language: textual conversation, resonate, reframe, reimagine.
Paper 2 · Section II

Module B: Critical study of literature

+ whole text + construction (form, style, structure) + your informed judgement of its value + critics (optional)
  • Personal voice is the differentiator: "For me, the play's enduring power lies in…" written formally.
  • Draw evidence from across the whole text, beginning to end. Show the text's arc.
  • If there's an extract, weave it into every paragraph, not just one.
  • Evaluate why the text is valued: its artistry, its distinctive qualities.
Paper 2 · Section III

Module C: The craft of writing

Part (a): your own piece   Part (b): Intent → Choice → Quote from your piece → Effect
  • Not an essay, but the reflection (part b) uses the same atom formula, applied to your own writing.
  • Write in the exact form the question asks for, and make the stimulus the core of the piece.
  • Practise 3–4 flexible pieces you can adapt, rather than one memorised story.

    Build a quote bank (the most useful thing you can make)

    Quote card = "quote" + technique + effect + idea (2–3 tags) + context hook

    Tag each quote with ideas (e.g. endurance, isolation, gender). In the exam, pick the tags that match the question and the quotes come with them. This is how top students adapt to any question without memorising an essay.

    Don't memorise one essay and hope. 2025 marker feedback explicitly warns against reproducing a prepared response. Memorise quote cards and paragraphs, then assemble to fit the question.
