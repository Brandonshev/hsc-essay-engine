Where Harwood fits

Harwood is your Common Module text (Texts and human experiences). In the HSC it's Paper 1, Section II: one 20-mark essay, about 40 minutes, on how your poems represent human experiences. The same question goes to English Standard students. The module asks how texts show identity, growth, acceptance, emotions, and the paradoxes of how people behave.

What markers want (the short version)

  • Answer the exact question, all the way through, not just in the intro.
  • Analyse, don't retell. "The child shoots an owl" = 0 marks. What Harwood does with it = marks.
  • Every quote gets an effect. Naming a technique alone is worth almost nothing.
  • Context goes inside sentences, not in a history paragraph.
  • Personal, confident voice. 2025 markers praised responses showing how the text deepened the student's own understanding.
  • Clear beats fancy. Markers flagged overly wordy writing as a problem.

The formula, simplified

You've probably heard of PEEL (Point, Evidence, Explain, Link) or TEEL. They're fine for structure, but "Explain" is vague, and vague is where marks leak. So this engine uses PQTEL: same skeleton, but "Explain" is broken into three exact steps: Quote, Technique, Effect.

T thesisP pointQ+T quote & techniqueE effectC contextL link
Formula 1 · Thesis
THESIS = IDEA + TWIST
Like you're 8: Say what Harwood shows about people (the IDEA). Then add a "but" (the TWIST). The "but" is what makes it smart. Without it, you've stated a fact. With it, you've made an argument.
Fill-in: "Harwood shows that [idea about people], but/yet [twist]."
✗ Idea only
"Harwood shows that growing up involves losing innocence."
✓ Idea + twist
"Harwood shows that growing up begins with the painful loss of childhood certainty, yet this loss becomes the source of a deeper compassion."
Formula 2 · Introduction
INTRO = Thesis + Context + Roadmap
Like you're 8: 3–4 sentences. T: your thesis. C: one sentence about Harwood's world that matters to your argument. R: name your 3 poems and what each one shows, in one sentence. If the question says "to what extent", end with "largely", "partly" or "entirely".
R template: "In poem A, ___; in poem B, ___; and in poem C, ___."
Formula 3 · QTE, the analysis unit
Quote → Technique → Effect   where  E = what it makes us see/feel + what it means about people
Like you're 8: Pick a few words from the poem (Q). Say what trick Harwood used (T). Then say what the trick does (E): first what it makes the reader picture or feel, then what that tells us about human experience. If you stop after T, you've only done a third of the job.
✗ Q + T only
"Harwood uses juxtaposition in 'ashes and loam'."
✓ Full QTE
"The juxtaposition of 'ashes and loam' [Q+T] places death and fertile soil side by side [E: see], suggesting that grief and renewal grow from the same ground [E: meaning]."

Rule: keep quotes short (2–8 words) and put them inside your own sentence. Long quotes waste time and hide your analysis.

Formula 4 · Body paragraph (PQTEL)
P + QTE + QTE + QTE + (context) + L
Like you're 8: P = one sentence saying what this paragraph proves (use the question's keyword and name the poem). Then do QTE three times, each quote proving a different bit of your point. Slip one context fact into a sentence. L = one sentence answering the question again.
  • Order the QTEs small → big: a single word → an image → the poem's structure or form.
  • One poem per paragraph is easiest. Strong essays often link a second poem in the last QTE.
  • ~250–300 words, about 8 minutes in the exam.
Formula 5 · Conclusion
CONCLUSION = Restate (new words) + So what?
Like you're 8: Say your thesis again in different words, squeezing your 3 poems into one sentence. Then finish with why it matters for people today. No new quotes. Never "In conclusion, I have shown".
Formula 6 · The 5-minute plan
PLAN = circle the keyword → IDEA + TWIST → pick 3 poems → 3 quotes each
Like you're 8: Find the question's main word. Write your thesis. Choose the 3 poems that fit that word best. Jot 3 quotes under each. Then write. The plan decides your mark more than your writing speed does.

Harwood's 7 poems

The prescribed poems are "The Glass Jar", "The Violets", "At Mornington", "Father and Child" (I "Barn Owl" and II "Nightfall"), "A Valediction", "Beyond Metaphor" and "The Sharpness of Death". Each card gives you the story in one line, the human experiences it shows, and short starter quotes.

Context hooks (slip one into each paragraph)

  • Born Gwendoline Foster in Brisbane in 1920; many poems return to a Queensland childhood.
  • A trained musician and organist. Music, Mozart and Beethoven run through her work.
  • Married Bill Harwood in 1945 and moved to Tasmania, where she lived until her death in 1995.
  • Fascinated by philosophy, especially Wittgenstein, and by how language can and can't hold experience.
  • Wrote under several pseudonyms (e.g. Walter Lehmann, Francis Geyer); famous for a prank on The Bulletin.
  • Christian upbringing shows in religious imagery: "blessed", "disciples", resurrection.
  • She said her poetry was sometimes "an attempt to keep off existential terror".

Some experiences change the way we see the world forever. To what extent does your prescribed text support this view?

Introduction · TCR

Gwen Harwood's poetry suggests that the experiences which change us most are moments when childhood certainty collapses, yet her poems also reveal that this loss can become the beginning of a deeper, more compassionate understanding. Drawing on her Brisbane childhood, her Christian upbringing and a lifelong fascination with philosophy, Harwood returns again and again to the threshold between innocence and experience. In "Barn Owl", a child's act of power becomes a confrontation with death; in "The Glass Jar", a child's faith in a talisman is betrayed; and in "Nightfall" and "The Violets", the adult speaker discovers that memory can turn these wounds into wisdom. The view is therefore largely true, though Harwood insists that what changes is not only how we see the world, but how we learn to live in it.

Body 1 · PQTEL · "Barn Owl"

In "Barn Owl", Harwood shows that a single experience can shatter a child's belief that she controls life and death. The opening, "Daybreak: the household slept. / I rose, blessed by the sun", uses religious imagery and a hushed caesura to cast the child as innocent and chosen, as if baptised by the morning light. This innocence is instantly undercut by the demonic metaphor "A horny fiend, I crept / out with my father's gun", which exposes the secret thrill of disobedience living inside the "blessed" child. In the barn she imagines herself "master of life and death, / a wisp-haired judge", and the irony of the tiny "wisp-haired" figure claiming godlike authority foreshadows her fall. When the owl does not die, the child who "believed death clean / and final" meets something "obscene", the enjambment spilling across the line break as her expectations spill into horror. Harwood once described her poetry as an attempt to keep off "existential terror", and here she dramatises the moment that terror first arrives. The experience permanently alters the child's view of the world: power, she learns, carries consequences that cannot be undone.

Body 2 · PQTEL · "The Glass Jar"

Similarly, "The Glass Jar" represents a transformative experience as the betrayal of a child's faith, revealing how quickly hope can harden into disillusionment. The boy "soaked / a glass jar in the reeling sun", the tactile verb "soaked" and the personified, "reeling" sun capturing his belief that light, and the safety it brings, can be stored and kept. Harwood's religious lexicon, naming the forces of the night "all the sun's disciples", elevates the jar into a sacred talisman, so that its failure becomes a crisis of faith. When that faith collapses, "hope fell headlong from its eagle height", the personification and plosive alliteration of "headlong" and "height" enacting the sudden drop from innocence into knowledge. By morning, the sun returns "to wink and laugh / in a glass jar beside a crumpled scarf", personification that turns the once-holy object into an emblem of mockery. The poem's discovery of the parents together invites a psychoanalytic reading, in which the boy is exiled from a child's exclusive world. Here too a single night changes the boy's world forever, though Harwood frames that change as loss rather than growth.

Body 3 · PQTEL · "Nightfall" + "The Violets"

However, in "Nightfall" and "The Violets" Harwood complicates this view, suggesting that experience changes us not only by wounding us but by teaching us to accept transience. "Nightfall" opens with "Forty years, lived or dreamed", the caesura blurring living and dreaming to show how memory reshapes time. The address "Old King, / your marvellous journey's done" alludes to King Lear, granting the dying father tragic dignity while the reversed roles, with the child now guiding the parent, complete the cycle begun at "Daybreak". The poem ends with the child "once quick / to mischief, grown to learn / what sorrows, in the end, / no words, no tears can mend", the parallel "no words, no tears" conceding limits the young "judge" in the barn could never accept. "The Violets" offers gentler consolation: the speaker kneels among "frail melancholy flowers among / ashes and loam", and the juxtaposition of ash and fertile loam holds death and renewal together, while her insistence that years cannot "distort those lamplit presences" suggests memory preserves love beyond loss. Harwood's reading of philosophers such as Wittgenstein shapes this search for what can still be held when certainty fails. The experience that changes us, then, is not simply the loss of innocence but the beginning of a lifelong reconciliation with it.

Conclusion · RS

Ultimately, Harwood's poetry affirms that some experiences change the way we see the world forever, but refuses to let that change be purely destructive. Through the child's confrontation with death in "Barn Owl", the betrayed faith of "The Glass Jar" and the reconciling memory of "Nightfall" and "The Violets", she traces a movement from shattered certainty to hard-won acceptance. For modern readers, her poems suggest that growing up means not escaping such moments, but learning to carry them with compassion.
